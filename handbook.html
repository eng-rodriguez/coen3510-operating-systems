1. Design decisions and open questions

Textbooks

Primary: Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces (OSTEP)
Reference: Operating System Concepts, 10th ed.
Kernel: xv6 RISC-V book and source

Platform

POSIX C, Ubuntu/Linux, GCC/Clang, Make, Git, GDB, QEMU and xv6. Python optional for data plots.

Teaching principle

Predict → observe → implement → test → explain. Concepts precede complex kernel changes; xv6 appears in Week 2.

Pending institutional validation

Instructor-adopted grading weights and assessment structure (Section 5) remain subject to final syllabus approval. Still pending: outcome targets, departmental ABET mapping and institutional policy review. No separate final exam is planned.

Course description: Design, implementation and evaluation of operating systems, emphasizing hardware/software interaction; system calls, processes, threads, scheduling, synchronization, memory, file systems, I/O, protection and isolation. Students implement and evaluate C user-space programs and bounded xv6 kernel changes.

Scope control: The co-requisite is Advanced Programming, not a completed C prerequisite. Administer a Week 1 diagnostic and offer a short C bridge; do not presume pointer proficiency.

3. Two-hour laboratory operating protocol

The single authoritative two-hour timeline is in Section 5 (introduction 0–15 min, team work 15–75 min, individual checkpoints 75–105 min, commit and push 105–120 min). This section gives the practical guidance behind it.

What must happen in class?

Require a minimum viable mechanism, one observed result, one individual explanation and an immutable checkpoint commit. For team work, each student answers an individual technical question. A student may show a partial implementation and still earn partial checkpoint credit by accurately diagnosing what fails.

What can happen at home?

Extended features, stress testing, benchmarks, documentation and cleanup. Home work is assessed with the instructor-provided local make test suite and a later short verification, not lengthy prose reports. Never require all code to be completed in the two-hour period.

Individual checkpoint rubric (6 points)

The individual checkpoint is worth 6 of the 10 lab points: three criteria, each scored 0–2. All criteria use the same levels: 2 = full (correct and independent), 1 = partial, 0 = no demonstrated competency. A “prompt” is a clarifying hint that does not give the answer. No AI is permitted.

Criterion2 — Full1 — Partial0 — None
A. Working artifact or observed resultRuns the required minimum artifact or reproduces the required observed result.Shows a partial artifact or meaningful intermediate state, or accurately diagnoses why it fails.No working artifact or result and no accurate diagnosis.
B. Prediction and explanationCorrectly predicts or explains the behavior without prompting.Explanation is partly correct, or correct only after one prompt.Explanation is incorrect or absent.
C. Independent unseen taskCompletes a small unseen change, diagnosis or trace correctly and independently.Completes it partially, with one prompt, or with a minor error.Cannot begin the task, or the result is incorrect.

Absence/accessibility: Provide equivalent supervised make-up demonstrations under an approved policy; ensure accommodations do not change the competency being assessed.

Scale: With 12 students in four teams of three, about two minutes per individual checkpoint takes roughly 24 minutes plus transitions, which fits the 30-minute checkpoint window. For larger sections, stagger checks during the work period, use short individual written traces, or add a teaching assistant. Do not promise every student a lengthy oral defense every week.

4. Project briefs and gates

Project A — Mini Unix Shell

Weeks 3–5 · Process lifecycle, descriptors, pipes, errors · integrated into graded laboratory units

Minimum specification: Launch commands; handle fork/exec/wait; one pipe; redirection; nonzero exit status

In-lab gates: Week 3: execute one command; Week 4: pipe/redirection; Week 5: unseen test and explanation

Home scope: Parser complexity and multi-stage pipelines can be completed at home; no full job-control requirement

Grading: Scored within the graded laboratory units using the standard lab rubric (individual checkpoint 60%, team local tests 30%, team Blackboard report 10%). The earlier internal rubric (tests 60%, defense 25%, design 15%) is superseded.

Project B — Concurrent Work Queue

Weeks 6–8 · pthreads, race conditions, invariants, condition variables · integrated into graded laboratory units

Minimum specification: Bounded queue; no lost work; clean shutdown; no busy wait

In-lab gates: Week 6: reproduce race; Week 7: working condition-variable wait; Week 8: stress test and lock-order explanation

Home scope: Additional load tests, benchmarks, cleanup and documentation

Grading: Scored within the graded laboratory units using the standard lab rubric (individual checkpoint 60%, team local tests 30%, team Blackboard report 10%). The earlier internal rubric (tests 55%, explanation 30%, evidence 15%) is superseded.

Project C — xv6 Kernel Extension

Weeks 10–15 · System-call interface, kernel/user boundary, testing, engineering design · final team project, 30% of course grade

Minimum specification: Implement one bounded process-info syscall OR scheduling instrumentation; document constraints

In-lab gates: Week 10 design gate; Week 11 kernel trace; Week 13 test demonstration; Week 15 unseen test and individual defense

Home scope: Implementation, regression tests, performance study, reproducibility README

Grading: Implementation 40%, technical report 25%, presentation 15%, individual defense 20% (Section 5; point breakdown in Section 5A). The earlier internal rubric (45/30/15/10) is superseded. Project C gate work is graded only here and is never also counted as a laboratory unit.

Universal project submission contract

Repository commit hash, build/run instructions, machine-readable test results, short design note (max one page), limitations, and a required AI-use disclosure. An instructor-selected unseen test and individual explanation verify ownership of understanding. Publish a local make test harness with clear failure messages.

Project dependency: A → B → C in increasing systems complexity; Project C must have a lower-risk approved option so students are not penalized for toolchain complexity rather than OS mastery.

Each team uses one private GitHub repository with starter code, README, Makefile, and tests. Teach clone, status, add, commit, pull, and push. Branches and pull requests are optional. The students decide how to distribute work; GitHub commit counts are not a proxy for individual learning or effort.

Final project: team deliverable, individual accountability

Require one team implementation, one Blackboard technical report (approximately 5–7 pages), and one presentation with a live demonstration. All students must participate in the presentation and individually explain part of the system during a no-AI defense. Project rubric: implementation and local tests 40%, technical report 25%, presentation/demo 15%, individual defense 20%. With four teams, allow 10–12 minutes per team presentation, including Q&A, plus separate individual technical defenses; adjust the schedule to actual enrollment.

AI policy

AI may assist team coding, debugging, planning, testing, and drafting. A concise AI-use disclosure is required in every team lab report and in the final project report. Students must verify outputs. No AI is permitted during individual supervised checkpoints, midterm, quizzes, or project defense unless specifically authorized or required by an accommodation. Do not use AI-detection scores as evidence of misconduct.

6. AI-use and academic-integrity design

Practice: AI permitted

Students may ask AI for explanations, debugging hints, test ideas and code suggestions. They must inspect, test and understand anything they submit.

Lab checkpoint: independent verification

During the individual checkpoint (about two minutes; Section 5), no AI assistance. Student predicts output, traces a path, fixes a small bug, or explains an invariant.

Individual exam: AI prohibited

Closed-AI supervised assessments test process tracing, scheduling calculations, concurrency reasoning, memory translation and design tradeoffs.

Required disclosure

Each team lab report and each project submission includes a concise AI-use statement: tool(s) used, task(s) assisted, and which generated suggestions were accepted or rejected. No requirement to submit entire chat transcripts by default; privacy and institutional policy take precedence.

Better than AI detection

Do not use AI-detector scores as proof of misconduct. Instead, use progressive commits, unseen tests, in-person explanation, small code perturbations, individual quizzes, and consistent rubrics. If a student cannot explain submitted work, grade the demonstrated learning outcome under a published policy; do not automatically infer misconduct.

Example individual check prompts

  • Predict how many times this line prints after two forks, and justify the possible output ordering.
  • Why must a condition-variable wait be inside a loop?
  • Which page-table entry bits explain this fault?
  • What happens to this pipe when the parent fails to close its write end?
  • Which test would distinguish a working scheduler from one that starves a process?
